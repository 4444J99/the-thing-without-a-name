On 20 June 2017 a dancer worked for an afternoon in a one-bedroom apartment in South Florida. The camera never moved. Against the wall, propped on the floor rather than hung, stood Dracula, The Invisible Man, The Phantom of the Opera and Creature from the Black Lagoon. That summer the photographer cut three of the frames into a flat composite by hand, choosing every edge by feeling, and then did nothing with them for nine years.

THE THING WITHOUT A NAME returns to the same afternoon with a machine. Because the camera was locked off, all one hundred sixty-two exposures are registered to each other and to the room, which means fragments of different photographs can be hung in space and still line up. The film opens on one untouched frame. The 2017 composite assembles itself, holds, and comes apart into a room that turns out to be deeper than a photograph. A body is built from thirteen different exposures, none of which contain it. Then the seed changes, four times, faster each time, and the piece restarts in front of you and is cut off mid-phrase.

The last four seconds are a number. This is not the film; it is one of them.
